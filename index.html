Australia, 2011-2021

Behind every road death, many more people are hospitalised

Road crash deaths make the headlines, but far more people are admitted to hospital with injuries that can change their lives. This story uses national hospital data to show how that burden changed over a decade and who carries it.

Who is this for? Anyone interested in road safety, including policy makers, health planners, researchers and the general public. All figures are raw counts, so please read About the data before comparing groups.

Trend over time

Hospitalised injuries rose about 16% over the decade

Each year, between 34,000 and 40,000 people were admitted to hospital after a road crash. The count grew from 34,033 in 2011 to 39,505 in 2021.

How to read this chart

Each dot is one year. Hover over a dot, or tab to it with the keyboard, to see the exact count. The vertical axis starts at zero so the change is not exaggerated.

What stands out

Numbers peaked at 39,866 in 2019, then fell to 37,966 in 2020, about 5% lower. They rebounded to 39,505 in 2021. The 2020 fall coincides with the first year of COVID-19, but this data alone cannot show why.

Keep in mind

A rising count does not necessarily mean roads became more dangerous. More people, and more travel, can also push totals up.

Road users

Motorcyclists and cyclists account for about 41% of injuries

Car drivers make up the largest single group, but people on motorbikes and bicycles together account for 167,684 hospitalisations over the decade.

How to read this chart

Each bar is the total for one type of road user from 2011 to 2021, longest first. Hover over a bar for the exact figure.

What stands out

Car drivers have 135,063 hospitalisations (about 33%). Motorcyclists follow with 92,322 and pedal cyclists with 75,362. Pedestrians account for 28,532.

Keep in mind

These are counts, not risk. We do not know how many people ride or drive, so this does not show who is most at risk per trip.

Who is affected

Men and people aged 40-64 are hospitalised most

Males account for about 65% of hospitalisations, and the 40-64 age group has the highest total.

How to read this chart

Each pair of bars is an age group, with female in blue and male in amber. Hover over a bar for the exact figure.

What stands out

Males: 266,763. Females: 144,106. The 40-64 group has 133,729 hospitalisations, followed by 26-39 (99,495) and 17-25 (88,925).

Keep in mind

Age bands are different widths. The 17-25 band covers nine years of age but the 40-64 band covers 25, so per year of age the younger group is much higher (about 9,900 vs 5,300 a year).

About the data

Where the numbers come from, and what they can't tell you

Source

Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics (BITRE), hospitalised injury data for 2011-2021, based on the AIHW National Hospital Morbidity Database.

What we counted

Hospitalisations where the cause of injury is a traffic (road crash) event. Counts exclude people who died in hospital. Our yearly totals match BITRE's published Table 1.

How we processed it

In KNIME we filtered the raw data to traffic injuries, then summed hospitalisations by year, remoteness, age group, sex, road user and counterparty (17,953 rows). TODO: add your cleaning decisions.

Raw counts, not rates

Figures are not adjusted for population, travel or distance driven, so comparing groups of different size can mislead. A bigger count does not mean a higher risk per person.

Missing and excluded records

15 hospitalisations have missing or indeterminate sex and 1 has a missing age group, so they are left out of the age and sex chart. Remoteness is missing for 8,833 hospitalisations.
